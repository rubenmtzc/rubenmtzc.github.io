    <button type="button" class="collapsible">Introduction to the module</button>
    <div class="content">
        <p>This module focuses on how individuals, firms, and institutions make decisions and interact in different economics environments. The module blends classical microeconomics theory with modern developments and applications, providing you with tools and empirical methods to understand and interpret real-world economic behaviour.</p>
        <p>We begin with the core building blocks of microeconomic analysis: consumer and producer theory. This covers how preferences, utility, and constraints shape consumer demand, and how firms set production strategies, and how we can derive the firm’s supply. We then study market outcomes from the interaction of demand and supply under competition and market power. We then cover richer environments where decision makers face uncertainty and intertemporal trade-offs.</p>
        <p>At this stage you are ready to apply the theory you have learnt to do consumer demand analysis. This section of the module is a bridge between the theory and the empirical analysis.</p>
        <p>The next part of the module covers strategic behaviour among economic agents. The study of strategic behaviour is what we commonly call game theory, a framework that offers a toolbox to analyse the behaviour of economic agents when they interact and their outcomes are interdependent. We will study both non-cooperative games and cooperative games.</p>
        <p>The final part of the module focuses on economic design. We will study platform markets, matching markets, and auctions. Here we will learn how the design of the rules and institutions becomes central, we will learn that network effects generate new pricing and operational strategies, how markets can be designed even when prices and money cannot be used, and how to create markets and competition when the goods transacted and the market participants  are considerably small, and when market information is scarce or even non-existent.</p>
        <p>By the end of the module, you will not only have mastered the theoretical foundations of these topics, but also gained exposure to empirical methods and applications, including demand estimation and computational tools. You are encouraged to think critically about the assumptions underlying the economic models and results that we will review, and to reflect on their relevance and limitations. The aim is to provide you with a toolkit that is rigorous, versatile, and applicable to both academic and policy and strategic contexts.</p>

    <div class="content">
        <h3>Topics</h3>
        <p>In week 1 we will start our coverage of consumer theory. We will first introduce the concept of preferences followed by the axiomatic definition of rational preferences. In a second step, we will define rational choices. This concept is important as choices are what we can observe, and this data can help us identify what are the preferences of the individuals. After these two steps we will introduce a key concept in economic theory: individual utility. Although it is a fictitious device, the utility concept is extremely useful to derive theoretical demand functions, and to support estimation methods for demands using data.Ok all that is very nice but we haven't talked about scarcity. Well, it turns out that we cannot always get what we want or in the quantities we want of it. Either because there is a limited number or because we have limited income or wealth, we need to restrict ourselves to what is available to us. To model this we introduce the budget set and the budget constraint, which will represent how much of any good or combination of goods a consumer can acquire given the resources available to them.We will probably stop here this week. After this week, we will be ready for the next set of topics in week 2. We will start with the constrained optimisation problem of the consumer, and other interesting topics.</p>

    <button type="button" class="collapsible">Week 2</button>
    <div class="content">
        <h3>Topics</h3>
        <p>This week, we will begin studying the constrained optimization problem of the consumer. We will continue building on utility theory, exploring indifference curves, and introducing the budget constraint—a concept that represents the consumer’s financial limitations. With these concepts in place, we’llmove on to a main objective: solving the constrained optimization problem, where the goal is to maximize utility subject to the budget constraint. The solution to this problem will give us the Marshallian demand functions, which will smoothly lead us to an important concept: elasticity. First, we’ll focus on price elasticity of demand, followed by income elasticity of demand.Next, we’ll study an alternative problem: expenditure minimization. Here, we will examine how a consumer minimizes expenditure while reaching a certain level of utility. From the solution to this problem, we’ll derive the Hicksian demand functions. We will also see that this problem is the dual of the utility maximization problem. Understanding this duality will pave the way for learning about the Slutsky Equation, where we’ll explore two key concepts: the income effect and the substitution effect.Finally, we’ll look into how to estimate the demand function and calculate the price elasticity of demand using real-world data and Python.</p>
        <h3>Readings</h3>
        <ul>
            <li>Chapters 3 and 4 of Serrano, R. (2018). A short course in intermediate microeconomics with calculus (A. Feldman, Ed.; Second edition.). Cambridge University Press.</li>
            <li>Chapter 5 of Osborne, M., & Rubinstein, A. (2023). Models in Microeconomic Theory: Expanded Second Edition. Open Book Publishers.</li>
        </ul>

    <div class="content">
        <h3>Topics</h3>
        <p>This week, we turn to the study of uncertainty and intertemporal choice. We will explore how individuals make decisions when outcomes are uncertain, and how risk preferences shape economic behaviour. We will also introduce the concept of expected utility and how it provides a tractable way to model choice under uncertainty. In addition, we will examine intertemporal choice, analysing how individuals trade off consumption and investment over time, including the role of discounting and present bias.As part of the week’s activities, you will watch Allison Schrager’s talk <em>Managing Risk in a More Uncertain World</em>, where she illustrates lessons on risk management with examples from sex workers, studs, and surfers.</p>
        <blockquote>Reflect on this video with your classmates: What did you learn? How do you personally manage risk? Are you risk-seeking in some areas of life but not others? Why?</blockquote>
